Cold snaps, thaws and spring rain in Park Hills

Water expands as it freezes, which is why a pipe can split long before it feels frozen. The split often shows up only at the thaw, when the ice melts and water pours through the crack. A wall that suddenly sounds like a faucet on a freezing night is the cue to close the main and call.

Spring brings the opposite problem. Saturated ground pushes water against basement walls, and a sump pump that sat idle all winter has to start cold. Test it before the first big storm, not during it.

A pipe in an unheated garage or a crawlspace corner is the usual victim. Insulating it takes minutes, and a split takes a plumber and a cleanup crew.

Season by season: what breaks in Park Hills homes

General patterns, not a promise about any one house.

Winter bursts

Pipes on exterior walls, in crawlspaces and in garages split first when temperatures stay low for a night or two.

Thaw floods

Water gushes when the ice plug melts, so check ceilings and walls after the temperature rises.

Spring seepage

Rain-saturated soil can bring water into a low basement and overwhelm a weak pump.

Summer irrigation leaks

A buried line that weeps all night wastes water quietly. The EPA says household leaks average more than 9,300 gallons per year.

Fall heater failures

Tanks past 10 to 15 years, per the US Department of Energy, often give out when the first cold week arrives.

On a frozen pipe that has not split, use a hair dryer on low. Never use an open flame.

Named for Devou Park, built on a hill

Park Hills was incorporated in 1927 and counted 3,162 residents and 1,493 housing units in 2020. The city takes its name from nearby Devou Park, the 700-acre hilltop park with its view of the Ohio River valley. Homes on a slope often have a basement wall below grade on one side, which makes drainage away from the house worth checking each autumn.

That hillside setting also affects which dispatcher questions matter most: which floor the water is on, and whether it reaches the lower level.
